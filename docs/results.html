Speedups measured on one CPU (median of 7 runs). Retraining itself takes only ~0.09 s here, so the speedup matters for larger pipelines. "5 seeds" = mean ± s.d.

How it changes with deletion size

Standard influence goes the wrong way in weights. For high-utilization deletions it ends further from retraining than doing nothing (all 5 seeds), though its predictions still improve.
PLU-GGN closes the gap. One step: 26× lower weight error than standard influence at 3%. Two steps: ~10−5.
Random deletions are easy. The pipeline barely moves, so the failure is specific to high-leverage patients.
Predictive quality is unchanged. PR-AUC differs by <0.002 across methods, far less than the ±0.02 between seeds.

Depth analysis: what a "membership audit" really measured

Our first privacy test scored each patient by how differently the deployed and the retrained preprocessor standardize them. It reported AUC 0.95–0.999 for "deleted vs. test patient" under a frozen preprocessor, and exactly 0.5 for PLU-GGN. Both numbers turned out to be misleading:

  • The score depends only on a patient's features, not on whether they were in training, so it cannot separate a member from a non-member with the same features.
  • It is identically zero when the deployed preprocessor equals the retrained one, so PLU-GGN's 0.5 holds by construction.
  • The deleted patients are the most extreme ones. Compared with ordinary test patients, extremeness alone gives a high AUC.

What is real is aggregate leakage: the frozen preprocessor still contains the deleted cohort's statistics. If old and new statistics are both exposed, subtracting them recovers the deleted cohort's mean number_inpatient exactly ( vs. for retained patients). PLU-GGN removes the first problem, not the second; that would need added noise.
