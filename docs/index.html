Forgetting the Pipeline Too

When a patient withdraws consent, a hospital model should behave as if that patient was never in the training data. Most unlearning methods update only the model weights, but tabular models also learn their preprocessing (imputation means, scaling, target encodings) from the data. This project measures what that costs, and fixes it.

1. Question

How closely do cheap unlearning updates match full retraining when the preprocessing is also learned from the data, and what does a frozen preprocessor leak?

The "gold standard" is oracle retraining: delete the patients, refit the whole pipeline and the model. Cheaper methods try to approximate it. The question is about unlearning fidelity, not about building the best readmission model.

One row
One inpatient encounter of a diabetic patient
Target
Readmitted within 30 days (11.2% positive)
Prediction time
At discharge: only pre-discharge counts and categories are used
Deletion request
The top q% of training encounters by utilization (3·inpatient visits + 0.1·lab procedures); default 3% = 599 encounters

2. Data

UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals (1999–2008, Strack et al. 2014; CC BY 4.0), via OpenML id 4541. We use a fixed random sample of of 101,766 encounters, from distinct patients. It is public and de-identified, so the sample is shipped with this site.

Features: 8 counts (time_in_hospital, num_lab_procedures, num_procedures, num_medications, prior-year number_outpatient/emergency/inpatient, number_diagnoses) and 4 categoricals (admission type, discharge disposition, medical specialty, A1C result).

Heavy right tails

Training set: median, 99th percentile and maximum of each count. The few extreme patients set the scaling.

Quality issues we handle

  • Repeat patients. Some patients have several encounters, so we split by patient: 0 patients appear in both train and test.
  • Missing specialty. of encounters have no medical specialty; kept as its own level.
  • Simulated missing counts. The released numeric columns are complete, so 12% of numeric cells are masked completely at random to exercise the imputer. This is a simulation; real missingness is rarely random.
  • Imbalance. 11.2% positives, so we use PR-AUC rather than accuracy for the model check.

3. Preprocessing (fitted on training rows only)

  • Numeric: mean imputation, then standardization z = (x − μ)/σ.
  • Categorical: Empirical-Bayes target encoding (Σy + 10·ȳ)/(n + 10), centred and scaled by 10.
  • Everything is stored as additive sufficient statistics (counts, sums, sums of squares), so removing rows means subtracting their statistics. That costs O(|Df|) and never touches the retained data.

4. Baselines and methods

Model: L2-regularized logistic regression (λ = 10, fixed in advance, not tuned on test). For each deletion request we compare against oracle retraining:

No unlearning

Keep the old weights and the old preprocessor. The trivial baseline.

Gradient ascent

5 small steps uphill on the deleted patients' loss. Preprocessor frozen.

Standard influence (GGN)

One Newton step that removes the deleted rows' gradient (Koh & Liang 2017, Guo et al. 2020). Preprocessor frozen.

PLU-GGN (ours)

(1) Exactly downdate the preprocessor. (2) Add the gradient change caused by the new coordinates, gshift. (3) Take a Gauss–Newton step in the new coordinates. Optional 2nd Newton step.

Why the frozen preprocessor hurts. Removing high-utilization patients shrinks σ for number_inpatient from 1.20 to 0.78. The same real-world effect then needs a smaller coefficient per standardized unit. Standard influence pushes that coefficient the other way, so it can end up further from retraining than doing nothing.

5. Evaluation design

  • Split: patient-grouped 80/20 (19,995 train / 5,005 test), fixed before any comparison. Test data is used only for reporting.
  • Weight error ‖θ − θ*r‖: the standard unlearning metric. Frozen-φ methods live in different coordinates, so we also report
  • Function-space error: mean KL divergence between the method's and the oracle's test predictions. This doesn't depend on coordinates.
  • Pipeline leak: how far the deployed μ, σ are from the retrained ones (100% = unchanged from before deletion).
  • Sanity check: test PR-AUC (prevalence baseline ; number_inpatient alone ).
  • Robustness: 5 seeds that change both the patient split and the missingness mask; a random-deletion control; deletion sizes from 0.5% to 8%.

Standard influence goes the wrong way in weights. For high-utilization deletions it ends further from retraining than doing nothing (all 5 seeds), though its predictions still improve.
PLU-GGN closes the gap. One step: 26× lower weight error than standard influence at 3%; two steps: ~10−5.
Random deletions are easy. The pipeline barely moves, so the failure is specific to high-leverage patients.

8. Depth analysis: what a "membership audit" really measured

Our first privacy test scored each patient by how differently the deployed and the retrained preprocessor standardize them, and reported AUC 0.95–0.999 for "deleted vs. test patient" under a frozen preprocessor, and exactly 0.5 for PLU-GGN. Both numbers turned out to be misleading:

  • The score depends only on a patient's features, not on whether they were in training, so it cannot separate a member from a non-member with the same features.
  • It is identically zero when the deployed preprocessor equals the retrained one, so PLU-GGN's 0.5 holds by construction.
  • The deleted patients are the most extreme ones. Compared with ordinary test patients, extremeness alone gives a high AUC.

What is real is aggregate leakage: the frozen preprocessor still contains the deleted cohort's statistics. If old and new statistics are both exposed, subtracting them recovers the deleted cohort's mean number_inpatient exactly ( vs. for retained patients). PLU-GGN removes the first problem, not the second; that would need added noise.

10. Limitations

  • Exact O(|Df|) downdates exist only for additive statistics; median or k-NN imputation would need approximations.
  • Only the preprocessor downdate is O(|Df|). The weight update makes one pass over the retained data: cheaper than retraining, but not free.
  • Missingness is simulated (completely at random).
  • One convex model with 13 parameters. For neural networks the Gauss–Newton step is only a local approximation.
  • One deletion rule. Other request patterns (e.g. all encounters of one patient) are untested.
  • No per-patient privacy claim (see §8). Associations in observational hospital data are not causal effects.

11. Conclusion

When preprocessing is learned, unlearning has to update it too. For additive statistics that is cheap and exact. Adding the pipeline-shift gradient brings a one- or two-step update to within 10−2–10−5 of retraining. Without it, standard influence can move weights the wrong way, on exactly the high-leverage patients where deletion matters most.
